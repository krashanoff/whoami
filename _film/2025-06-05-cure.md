---
title: Cure (Kurosawa; 1997)
---

That the film "Cure" saw acclaim is a miracle. The actors, at time of filming, didn't truly understand
their purpose in the film. The director didn't seem to have a strong plan, and the story
itself is _extremely_ ambiguous. And yet, in spite of it all, I walked away from the movie
feeling a profound depth of message.

In interviews with Hagiwara Masato and Yakusho Kouji, neither actor has a deep resonance with
their role nor a method to the way they play the story. The director himself, Kurosawa
Kiyoshi, does not have a strong, immediate message that he wants to put forth. He merely
has ideas that he wants to explore. However, unlike Inland Empire, I found it had enough
of a structure to guide the viewer through their own headspace that it was digestible.

I can't tell you directly what it is about Cure that resonated with me to my core, it's the
sort of thing you have to experience for yourself. Every
time I think about it, something in its psychotic hypnosis and lucid dreamscapes arrest me
in a ruminant malaise. Its content is immensely dark and disturbing;
at least it feels to me that it should. No, the sane part of me knows it should. But even
so, in knowing that it should feel one way, when I think about it -- when I reflect on
the emotion that washed over me when the credits began, and the Gypsy's Song began to play --
and I struggle to put my thoughts into words, all I can feel... Is calm.

An overwhelming, immense sense of calm. It got up and walked off, gone from my mind. Like a
confessional. I can't tell you what Cure left me with, because it scares me to admit.
I know what it is. And I know that I shouldn't have taken it as a good thing. But I did.

I did. And I can't help it. The part of me that understands Cure is _the real me_. I live
every day in spite of this, and that's what frightened me.

I can't tell you what Cure told me, because it's not what it will tell you.

Cure is a horror movie. It is a psychological movie; viewers remark it is hypnotic and
entrancing, as are its hallmark themes. But it isn't scary, not to me. The content of Cure didn't
scare me. It made me reflect on myself; reflect on the me I deny.

In the modern era, each and every one of us lives in a conscious, self-induced denial of the
hard realities of our lives. We know this, subconsciously, and will opportunistically search
for excuses to act on those subconscious realizations. Cure explores this tendency, whether
the director intended to or not.
